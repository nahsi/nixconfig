# Research methodology

Adapted from the upstream reference identified in [the skill](../SKILL.md).

## Start broad, then go specific

Build a foundation before choosing candidates. Overview articles, surveys, Wikipedia references, and curated indexes can reveal the vocabulary and categories of an unfamiliar field. Treat their organization as a provisional map, not an exhaustive taxonomy.

Once the map has useful distinctions, read the sources that resolve them: original documentation, implementations, papers, independent comparisons, practitioner accounts, or historical records. Broaden again if the evidence suggests the original framing missed a family of approaches.

## Following trails

When you find a valuable source:

1. Inspect its references and linked resources. What did the author or commenter think was worth naming?
2. Search unfamiliar names or mechanisms that could change the decision. Preserve the originating URL so the lead is traceable.
3. Browse neighboring categories in a relevant awesome list, bibliography, or directory. Category labels may reveal an approach you did not know to query for.
4. Follow an informative author's other work or later work citing a source when that helps answer an open question.
5. Check the source behind a repeated claim. Several summaries citing one report are one evidence chain.
6. Update the working map: new approach, relation to existing options, tradeoff, contradiction, or remaining question.

A lead can be worth investigating without its originating claim being credible. Verify consequential conclusions at the source that can establish them.

## Forum and discussion research

- Find relevant discussions, then read the replies. A useful alternative may appear below a popular top-level post.
- Distinguish users describing their own setup from recommendations, launch promotion, speculation, and summaries of someone else's experience.
- Check that the name identifies the intended project. A general concept and several implementations can share a name.
- Follow named tools, workarounds, linked repositories, and references into their own documentation and discussions when they could change the answer.
- For a bug report, check the environment, date, response, and linked resolution. A closed issue is not automatically a fix; an open issue does not prove the current release is affected.
- Preserve contrasting experiences and their conditions. Popularity, votes, and complaint counts in a selected thread do not measure adoption or reliability.

Curated lists are discovery indexes, not endorsements. Read the project and relevant experience reports before recommending an entry.

## Query techniques

Choose queries from the current questions and observed leads:

- exact phrases for names or quotations;
- synonyms and adjacent-domain terminology for missing approaches;
- site/domain restrictions for appropriate communities or primary sources;
- date/version constraints when the behavior is time-sensitive;
- comparison, migration, failure, or limitation terms to find counterevidence;
- backward and forward reference searches for intellectual lineage.

If results are sparse, try a different vocabulary or source family before concluding that evidence is scarce. Report coverage limits when a platform is blocked or only snippets are available. Use another lawful accessible source rather than treating access failure as negative evidence.

## Source triangulation

Check independence and fitness, not a fixed number of links. One specification may settle a documented option; a general adoption claim needs broader evidence. Trace citations to their origin and identify sources repeating the same announcement.

Read [source evaluation](sources.md) when deciding what a source actually establishes.

## Working notes and stopping

Keep a compact record while investigating:

- Goal and current framing.
- Approaches discovered, with defining sources and tradeoffs.
- Promising leads: originating source, open question, next action.
- Leads followed or dropped, with the finding or reason.
- Claims supported, disputed, or still unknown.

Stop for decision sufficiency, saturation, diminishing returns, or the assigned limit. Retain important unfinished leads and the reason for stopping. Do not equate a polished answer or a large bibliography with coverage.
