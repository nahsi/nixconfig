# Source evaluation

Adapted from the upstream reference identified in [the skill](../SKILL.md). Assess evidence for the particular claim, not the prestige of the publishing platform.

## Discovery value and evidence strength

A source has two independent uses:

- **Discovery:** suggests a tool, term, category, author, alternative, or question worth pursuing.
- **Evidence:** supports a specific claim after its content, context, and limitations have been inspected.

A forum aside can be an excellent discovery source while its performance claim remains unverified. A maintainer's personal blog can be primary evidence about their design. A peer-reviewed paper may say nothing about current practitioner adoption.

## Match the source to the claim

| Claim | Useful evidence | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Documented behavior or availability | Current official documentation, release notes, source code | Ease of use or independent performance |
| A user's operational experience | First-hand discussion, issue reproduction, migration account | Prevalence across users or cause without further evidence |
| A bug was fixed | Linked change/release and confirmation of the affected scenario | That every related problem is fixed |
| Adoption or satisfaction | Independent use reports with context, transparent surveys or usage data | Stars, forks, votes, and promotional posts alone are not usage counts |
| Comparative performance | Reproducible setup, relevant workloads, fair baselines, limitations | Transfer to different versions, hardware, or workloads |
| Scientific or historical conclusion | Original evidence and appropriate independent synthesis | Publication venue alone does not establish validity |

For consequential regulated-domain claims, use appropriate current authoritative evidence and state where qualified review is required. This skill does not supply professional authority.

## Evaluation checklist

For a decision-critical claim, ask:

- **Proximity:** did the author observe, build, measure, or merely repeat it?
- **Context:** which version, date, environment, population, and use case?
- **Evidence:** is the supporting passage accessible; are methods and limitations shown?
- **Purpose and bias:** promotional, affiliate, sponsored, advocacy, or independent? First-party facts can still be useful.
- **Corroboration:** do genuinely independent sources agree, and under which conditions?
- **Counterevidence:** what would invalidate the claim; is there a relevant failure report or alternative explanation?

Keep direct observations, author claims, and your inferences distinguishable. Quote accurately and link to the specific evidence when possible.

## Common traps

- **Repeated announcement as consensus:** follow copies back to the original.
- **Complaint volume as failure rate:** the denominator and selection process are usually unknown.
- **Popularity as technical quality:** votes can prioritize reading, not settle correctness.
- **Age as automatic rejection:** check whether the mechanism or reported behavior changed.
- **Authority outside its scope:** a vendor can establish a feature without establishing market preference.
- **Small or biased samples:** qualify conclusions rather than inflating them into general adoption claims.
- **Correlation as causation:** preserve plausible confounders and the limits of the measurement.
- **Invented connections:** distinguish a useful analogy from an evidenced relationship.
- **Blocked source as absence:** inaccessible pages and failed searches leave a coverage gap.

## Communicating uncertainty

State what the evidence establishes and what remains unknown. For example: "Two users report this on version X; I did not find evidence of its prevalence or a confirmed fix." Do not compress different claims into one confidence score.

For contradictions, retain both accounts and investigate differences in setup, date, or meaning. If the conflict remains unresolved, report it. For translations or archived copies, identify that limitation.
